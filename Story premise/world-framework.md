# Echoes of Alderfall: Setting and Story Bible

Status: Third creative draft, defining the target setting for NPC dialogue, companion stories, faith, regional creatures, and the main campaign. This is the target setting, not a description of everything currently implemented.

The direction is an original fantasy world inspired by real regions and their folklore. Its countries, cultures, gods, and history are fictional. Inspiration should shape the landscape, institutions, supernatural encounters, and ordinary lives of its inhabitants.

This draft supersedes the first draft's assumption that existing story content must remain intact. Existing characters and places are material to adapt; their current dialogue and quest explanations do not constrain the new fiction. Major proposed changes are identified below. The companion document, [Narrative Rewrite Brief](narrative-rewrite-brief.md), maps this setting to the existing game and provides replacement story and dialogue examples.

### How to use this document

Playable main-story checkpoint, 22 September 2026: [the campaign rewrite pass](../Java/docs/main-story-rewrite-pass.md) connects all 26 current quests to the premise in sections 14–17. Northern oath inspections and the southern guest-fire release now provide local, observable examples of protection turned into compulsion. This establishes those cases, not universal player knowledge of the author-facing history below. The eastern voyage, new folklore bosses, and final network settlement remain targets.

- Sections 1–4 establish the shared world and the distinction between current content and target fiction.

## 1. The world in one paragraph

Alderfall is a land where settlements survive through agreements: between neighbors, between kingdoms, and between people and the powers that inhabit their surroundings. In the north, those agreements are remembered in stone, song, and ancestral obligations. In the south, they govern wells, hospitality, and dealings with beings of fire and wind. Western kingdoms inherit bargains with the inhabitants of ancient woods. In the central lands, household customs and village festivals preserve protections that royal archives have partly forgotten. Farther east, mountain sanctuaries and coastal communities maintain relationships with local spirits and the dead. As the wards fail, each region discovers that its familiar stories contain both practical warnings and uncomfortable omissions.

The intended tone is **adventurous folklore with moments of dread, warmth, and wonder**. A haunted mill, a generous innkeeper, an impossible market, and a dangerous royal secret should all belong to the same world.

## 2. Foundations and continuity

### Existing story anchors

The current project already establishes Oathstead Camp, damaged wards, old oaths, concealed records of the Demon War, Vaelthara, twelve socket stones, and the Old Gate leading to the Hollow Throne. Retain these as recognizable starting points, then change their meaning and supporting stories to serve the new setting. Section 14 defines the proposed replacement premise.

The current map names six kingdoms or political territories:

| Existing territory | Role in this framework |
| --- | --- |
| Highwall March | Northern mountain frontier; strongest inland expression of the northern tradition. |
| Northroad Freeholds | Northern and northeastern crossroads, with cairn villages and eastern harbors; a bridge toward the farther east. |
| Sanctum Sunrealm | Southern heartland of caravan routes, irrigated settlements, sun shrines, and desert ruins. |
| Riverside Reach | Western river kingdom, where bridge keeps and trade towns meet enchanted woodland. |
| Crownlands of Alderfall | Central heartland of archives, old royal authority, villages, and forest folklore. |
| Belltower Fenlands | A distinct marsh culture with bells, waterways, drowned histories, and connections to eastern trade. |

These are political borders. The five broad cultural regions below can cross them. A northern fishing town and Highwall fortress may share stories while disagreeing about laws, rulers, and religion.

**Target resolution of an existing inconsistency:** the current map defines six territories while the main quest refers to five kingdoms. In the revised fiction, there are five mainland treaty signatories, the independent Northroad Freeholds, and the eastern Lantern Isles. Call the present-day alliance the gathering of realms, not the five kingdoms. Freehold and eastern representatives participate in the rewritten campaign. This is a planned narrative and quest change, not an implemented fix.

The Japanese-inspired lands lie beyond the existing eastern coast and are called **the Lantern Isles** in this draft. They are a full participant in the target campaign, with their own settlements, conflicts, and representative. Their addition requires new content and a revised route through the twelve-stone campaign. The Fenlands and Freeholds keep their distinct identities.

### Creative rules

1. **Folklore changes ordinary life.** It affects bridges, funerals, planting, travel, meals, and household arrangements.
2. **Each region contains disagreement.** Merchants, farmers, rulers, religious communities, and younger generations have different interests.
3. **Myth is evidence, not a perfect instruction manual.** A tale can preserve a useful warning and misidentify its cause.
4. **The supernatural has local rules.** Players can observe, investigate, and learn them before making a costly choice.
5. **Culture is learned and shared.** Birthplace or ancestry does not determine morality, profession, or magical ability.
6. **Every region has ordinary kindness and ordinary wrongdoing.** None exists solely as an enemy land, spiritual paradise, or source of exotic goods.

## 3. Geography and connections

The broad arrangement is a temperate central basin opening west toward river mouths and a stormy coast. Northern mountains feed its rivers. Southward, grasslands become dry plateaus, irrigated valleys, and desert. Eastern wetlands lead to coastal ports and a sea crossing to the Lantern Isles.

This is a proposed geographic framework, not a map of current tile coordinates. Borders should follow rivers, passes, watersheds, and negotiated claims rather than straight cultural divisions.

| Region | Main inspiration | Signature question | First impression |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| North: Stormbound Holds | Norse mythology and Scandinavian folklore | What do the living owe the dead? | A harbor bell rings as a crew returns with one passenger too many. |
| South: Sunrealm and Well Roads | Arabian folklore and storytelling | When does a promise become a prison? | A public fountain flows uphill while its keeper argues with an unseen guest. |
| West: River and Thorn Kingdoms | Western European fairy tales, with Celtic and Arthurian influences | What makes a ruler's claim legitimate? | A toll bridge admits travelers but refuses the local lord. |
| Center: Hearth and Crownlands | Central European village and forest folklore, with Germanic and Slavic influences | Who protects a home when official protections fail? | Every hearth in a street goes cold except the abandoned house. |
| East: Lantern Isles | Japanese folklore and supernatural storytelling | How should people live beside powers they cannot own? | Festival lanterns drift against the tide toward a sealed shrine. |

Stormbound Holds, Well Roads, River and Thorn Kingdoms, and Hearth and Crownlands are working regional descriptions. They do not replace existing kingdom names.

Travel creates overlap. A southern glassmaker can live in Archive City; a northern crew can maintain an eastern harbor shrine; a western village can borrow a central harvest custom. Mixed communities develop their own traditions rather than choosing one complete regional identity.

## 4. A shared supernatural foundation

The following are proposed author-facing rules. Characters may explain them differently.

### Places, memory, and obligation

Some places and objects accumulate supernatural presence through long use, strong attachment, repeated ritual, or direct contact with a powerful being. Age alone does not make every stone or tool sentient.

A spirit may belong to a particular river, household, mountain, or craft. Others travel or maintain societies independent of human settlements. Their needs and intentions vary; unusual appearance does not establish hostility.

An ordinary promise is not automatically a spell. A binding supernatural oath requires an identifiable witness or power, an intentional act, and terms that can be discovered. The danger comes from misunderstood terms, coercion, lost records, or changes in the circumstances under which an agreement was made.

### Wards

Wards have physical anchors and ongoing requirements. A carved stone, maintained bell, tended grove, or protected well may anchor one. Some depend on a negotiated relationship; others use craft, materials, and repeated maintenance. This gives different regions practical reasons to cooperate without making all their traditions identical.

Ward failure can have several causes: damaged anchors, neglected upkeep, stolen offerings, deliberate sabotage, or a bargain that no longer serves the people bound by it. Repairing the object may solve only part of the problem.

### The dead, gods, and demons

The dead do not all return. When a haunting occurs, investigate the specific attachment, injustice, magical interference, or interrupted rite. Different funeral traditions can work without one culture holding the only correct account of death.

Gods and great powers remain partly uncertain. Their cults have histories, rival interpretations, and regional names. Use original deities; section 12 introduces several whose worship has practical consequences for communities.

Vaelthara and the established demonic threat retain their own place in the story. A southern spirit, eastern apparition, or northern ancestor is not automatically a demon. People in-world may make that mistake, and quests can expose its consequences.

Magic always needs a means and has a limit. A river spirit can influence its own waters; it cannot casually end a distant drought. A binding seal needs a suitable vessel and upkeep. Major restoration should leave a material or social consequence rather than resetting everything without cost.

## 5. North: The Stormbound Holds

**Identity:** Seafaring communities, mountain freeholds, ancestral claims, dangerous winters, and skilled makers. Norse divine stories and Scandinavian folk traditions provide a palette; local names and institutions belong to Alderfall. The *Prose Edda* is a starting reference for giants, divine conflicts, and extraordinary crafted objects. [Source](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18947/18947-h/18947-h.htm)

**Landscape and life:** Fjords, pine valleys, upland grazing, rocky islands, and short but productive summers. Fishing, farming, weaving, ship repair, and trade sustain the region. Feasting halls also host negotiations and shelter travelers.

**Local supernatural logic:** The proposed northern traditions place weight on witnessed deeds and accurate remembrance. A memorial that names only a victorious chief may leave the sailors who saved the village unacknowledged. A burial mound can be a protected neighbor, a disputed inheritance, or a dangerous prison.

**Proposed powers and factions:**

- The **Hearth Assemblies** settle local disputes and organize winter stores.
- The **Keel Houses** control ships and trading partnerships; some profit from raiding, while others need dependable peace.
- The **Cairn Keepers** preserve burial records and maintain protections along mountain roads.

**Creature palette:** Barrow revenants, sea apparitions, mountain giants, hidden neighbors, and guardian beasts. Giants can have territory and grievances; revenants can be witnesses as well as enemies. These are encounter directions, not interchangeable combat classes.

**Everyday custom, invented for this setting:** Before winter, households publicly account for anyone lost under their protection. Omitting a name is both a civic offense and a possible source of haunting.

**Quest seed — The Uncounted Crew:** A wreck returns with the tide each night. Its dead crew demands the wages their captain concealed. Recover the ship's account, identify surviving dependents, and decide how restitution should be paid when the captain's descendants now maintain the town's only rescue boat.

**Visual language:** Weathered timber, turf roofs, carved posts, wool, iron fittings, sailcloth, and cairns. Separate wealthy ports, working farms, and frontier forts in their silhouettes and materials.

**Existing connections:** Highwall March, Snowrest, Frosthollow, and the northern traditions of the Freeholds. New coastal holds can broaden the region beyond the current mountain content.

## 6. South: The Sunrealm and the Well Roads

**Identity:** Caravan cities, oasis communities, maritime trade, poetry, astronomy, hospitality, and negotiations with powerful unseen beings. Arabian folklore is the main direction. The *Arabian Nights* supplies narrative starting points such as dangerous encounters, sealed supernatural beings, and bargains; it is a literary collection, not a complete account of a single society. [Source](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20916/pg20916-images.html)

**Landscape and life:** Date gardens, irrigated valleys, stony plateaus, salt flats, dry grasslands, desert routes, and busy ports. Water engineers, farmers, sailors, scribes, and craftspeople matter as much as caravan leaders.

**Local supernatural logic:** Wells and roads carry layered rights: who maintains them, who may draw from them, and which promises secured their protection. Inspired by jinn stories, independent beings of fire and wind can be hosts, rivals, patrons, or dangerous captives. Their roles extend beyond granting wishes.

**Proposed powers and factions:**

- The **Well Councils** allocate water and maintain channels; their decisions can protect communities or entrench privilege.
- The **Caravan Houses** finance long routes and dispute tolls, escorts, and obligations to stranded travelers.
- The **Keepers of the Open Door** maintain guesthouses and mediate conflicts involving sanctuary.

**Everyday custom, invented for this setting:** A traveler receives a first cup of water before negotiations begin. Denying it publicly declares that the host refuses the protection of guest law.

**Creature palette:** Wind travelers, fire beings, ruin-haunting deceivers, enormous nesting birds, and guardians bound to vessels. Give each its own motive and relationship to the local ecology.

**Quest seed — The Thirteenth Cup:** A town's well draws water through a captive spirit. The original contract promised release after twelve generations; the council secretly changed the genealogy. Freeing the spirit immediately threatens the harvest. Expose the alteration, secure another water source, and negotiate a transition the spirit has reason to accept.

**Visual language:** Shaded courtyards, stone and earthen buildings, tiled water channels, woven awnings, astronomical instruments, glasswork, and regional textiles. The Sanctum's sun imagery is one religious tradition within this varied region.

**Existing connections:** Sanctum Sunrealm, the Sunken Shrine, caravan roads, and Solari's established story. Persian or other neighboring inspirations can support specific future communities when deliberately researched and identified.

## 7. West: The River and Thorn Kingdoms

**Identity:** River baronies, contested crowns, enchanted woods, chivalric expectations, and dangerous generosity. Western European fairy tales provide the broad direction, with Celtic and Arthurian influences for selected stories. Collections such as Joseph Jacobs's *Celtic Fairy Tales* offer individual tales to study rather than one uniform mythology. [Source](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7885)

**Landscape and life:** Navigable rivers, orchards, wet meadows, wooded hills, old roads, market towns, and castles that depend on the surrounding farms. Ferries and mills can matter more than royal ceremonies.

**Local supernatural logic:** Territorial rights sometimes descend from bargains with older inhabitants. A crown may possess the written charter but omit the obligation that made it valid. Hospitality, gifts, and invitations can create specific obligations when a supernatural party is involved.

**Proposed powers and factions:**

- The **Bridge Courts** unite river barons, toll officials, and rival claimants.
- The **Thorn Compacts** are local agreements between settlements and neighboring otherworldly powers; their keepers disagree about how much may be renegotiated.
- The **Road Orders** protect pilgrims and merchants, with tensions between wealthy sponsors and the people using the roads.

**Everyday custom, invented for this setting:** A host states whether a gift is freely given or offered in exchange. This is usually ordinary courtesy; at certain woodland crossings, the distinction becomes binding.

**Creature palette:** Enchanted beasts, water horses, wandering knights under curses, cunning woodland neighbors, and local dragons. An apparent monster may be enforcing a valid agreement through unacceptable means.

**Quest seed — The Bridge That Refused a King:** A bridge turns the lord's soldiers back but lets everyone else pass. The founding charter promised free passage during famine, and the lord has imposed a grain toll. Recover the original terms and decide whether to restore the charter, negotiate new duties, or replace the crossing at considerable local cost.

**Visual language:** Pale stone, timber towns, heraldic cloth, thorn hedges, weathered roadside shrines, mossy ruins, and worked riverbanks. Beauty should coexist with mud, labor, and repair.

**Existing connections:** Riverside Reach, Briarbridge, Mirella, and the existing emphasis on supplies, tolls, and river power.

## 8. Center: The Hearth and Crownlands

**Identity:** Forest villages, household protections, craft towns, royal bureaucracy, and seasonal obligations. Central European folklore is the main direction, with Germanic and Slavic traditions developed through specific local communities. Grimm's tales provide an initial reference for uncanny households, transformations, difficult tasks, and encounters governed by unexpected rules. [Source](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2591/pg2591-images.html)

**Landscape and life:** Broadleaf forests, grain fields, orchards, rivers, marsh margins, mining hills, and cities that record and tax the surrounding countryside. The center has institutions and ordinary customs of its own; it is not culturally neutral ground.

**Local supernatural logic:** Repeated household and seasonal practices can maintain small protections. The person who tends an oven, cleans a threshold, or remembers a burial may know something absent from official records. Some customs have been misunderstood or used to justify cruelty and deserve investigation.

**Proposed powers and factions:**

- The **Royal Archive** preserves useful knowledge while carrying the consequences of censorship and missing records.
- The **Hearth Guilds** connect craftspeople who know the protective traditions of their trades.
- The **Boundary Stewards** maintain forest edges and village paths, often disputing royal grants of land.

**Everyday custom, invented for this setting:** When a family moves, the outgoing and incoming households share the first meal at the hearth. The rite transfers responsibility for the house's protections; towns now struggle to adapt it for lodgers and empty properties.

**Creature palette:** Household spirits, forest wardens, restless dead, mill apparitions, and witches with individual practices and loyalties. Specific Slavic beings should receive their own researched treatment when introduced, rather than being renamed copies of neighboring creatures.

**Quest seed — The Cold Street:** A landlord demolished a shared bakehouse and scattered its hearthstones. Food spoils throughout the street, while the abandoned caretaker's house stays warm. Locate the stones, investigate the displaced guardian, and arrange a new shared hearth or another sustainable protection.

**Visual language:** Plaster and timber, carved lintels, tiled stoves, communal ovens, orchard fences, market squares, and forest-edge shrines. Royal buildings and village homes express different kinds of authority.

**Existing connections:** Crownlands, Archive City, the orchard ward, and Oathstead. The Fenlands retain a separate emphasis on flood knowledge, bell networks, reed shrines, and the drowned dead. Their traditions overlap with the center without being reduced to a variant of it.

## 9. East: The Lantern Isles

**Identity:** Island towns, cultivated valleys, mountain sanctuaries, working ports, seasonal festivals, and close encounters with strange beings. Japanese folklore is the main inspiration. Hearn's *Kwaidan* offers starting stories involving apparitions and encounters, including snow and ghost narratives; its individual retellings do not define all Japanese belief. [Source](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1210/1210-h/1210-h.htm)

**Landscape and life:** Forested mountains, terraced fields, river plains, fishing settlements, storm coasts, volcanic springs, and dense trading towns. Farmers, ferrymen, artisans, performers, and merchants should appear as frequently as warriors and shrine attendants.

**Local supernatural logic:** In this proposed region, relationships with particular places require attention to their specific histories. A mountain guardian, an animal trickster, a neglected object, and a human ghost are different kinds of encounters. Real-world terms such as kami and yokai should not become synonyms for generic monsters.

**Proposed powers and factions:**

- The **Harbor League** coordinates pilots, trade, and storm relief while resisting inland toll claims.
- The **Mountain Sanctuaries** preserve distinct local agreements; they do not all answer to one central priesthood.
- The **Provincial Houses** compete for authority, with differing approaches to land, labor, and spiritual obligations.

**Everyday custom, invented for this setting:** Before a major bridge repair, workers carry a small light along the old crossing and then the new one. It announces the changed route to the beings believed to travel beside people.

**Creature palette:** Foxlike deceivers or allies, river beings, winter apparitions, mountain challengers, and old objects with unexpected agency. Whether an encounter ends in combat depends on its particular history and behavior.

**Quest seed — Lanterns Against the Tide:** Festival lights gather above a submerged village rather than drifting out to sea. Official memorials list the village as evacuated, but workers were left behind when a reservoir gate closed. Recover their names, confront the surviving records office, and choose how to restore remembrance while protecting the farms that now depend on the reservoir.

**Visual language:** Timber joinery, plaster storehouses, tiled roofs, weathered harbor structures, cloth signs, stone paths, and seasonal planting. Develop contrasts between busy streets, practical farm buildings, and mountain sites.

**Campaign connections:** Eastern travelers appear in Freehold and Fenland ports early in the story. A crossing to the Isles becomes part of the revised main campaign, where the player learns how the old ward network crosses the sea. Section 15 assigns an existing stone to this new chapter; the campaign still uses twelve stones in total.

## 10. Shared history and present tensions

Use a relative history until dates and dynasties are needed:

1. **Local foundations:** Communities establish routes, settlements, protections, and agreements independently. No single empire invents every tradition.
2. **Growing exchange:** Trade and migration spread techniques and stories. Similar customs acquire different meanings in different places.
3. **The Demon War:** A coalition stops an invasion through the Hollow Throne. Its members contribute different forms of magic and remember different sacrifices.
4. **The concealed settlement:** The victors install a network that can compel spirits to maintain the wards. Public histories celebrate the victory and omit how emergency bindings became permanent. Section 14 defines this proposed central revelation.
5. **The present failure:** Vaelthara exploits damaged protections and fractured trust. Local neglect, exploitation, and older grievances also produce problems independently of her campaign.

Present relationships should create reasons to travel. Northern ports need southern salt and central grain. Southern builders buy timber and metal while exporting glasswork and engineering knowledge. Western tolls can interrupt everyone's supply routes. Eastern pilots bring ships through waters that mainland captains do not know. The Fenlands control crossings and flood expertise that larger neighbors repeatedly undervalue.

Oathstead becomes important because people can make workable new agreements there. Settlement growth should reveal this through visiting artisans, shared facilities, disputes over customs, and small acts of cooperation. No single imported ritual solves every problem.

## 11. Turning folklore into playable stories

For each new location, define:

| Element | Required question |
| --- | --- |
| Ordinary need | What do people need here: food, water, safe travel, shelter, work, or recognition? |
| Local story | What explanation do inhabitants give for the supernatural problem? |
| Visible evidence | What can the player actually inspect, hear, recover, or witness? |
| Discoverable rule | What behavior or condition makes the phenomenon change? |
| Competing interests | Who benefits from the current arrangement, and who pays for it? |
| Player decision | Which workable responses have different consequences? |
| Lasting result | What changes in the place, its inhabitants, or its relationships afterward? |

Not every encounter needs moral ambiguity. A malicious creature can require a straightforward defense. Others may call for negotiation, repair, investigation, trickery, or leaving a protected place alone. Learning a folk rule should sometimes change a battle's preparation or objective.

Keep dialogue concrete: explain what happened and what the player can do before adding poetic meaning. Reveal beliefs through actions and disagreements rather than having every resident recite the same lore.

Regional texture should also appear in small moments: travelers sharing a meal, children debating a ghost story, a craftsperson repairing a charm, a skeptic following one family custom, or a festival continuing despite rain.

## 12. Original fantasy identities

All names, powers, and customs introduced from this point onward are original proposed setting material. The real-world references above describe inspiration; the fiction below is not presented as historical mythology.

### North: Skeldreach and the Stormbound Holds

Skeldreach is the broad northern homeland. Highwall guards its inland passes; the coastal Holds elect war leaders for particular campaigns, while local assemblies control stores, land disputes, and winter shelter. Northroad communities connect this northern culture with eastern shipping and have their own independent councils.

The aurora sometimes illuminates the **Wake Road**, a passage traveled by ancestral ships. Most carry their passengers onward. A ship that anchors above a village usually means that something has interrupted its voyage. The **Rimewrights**, ancient mountain smiths, trade extraordinary work for scarce materials, skilled service, or memories willingly given. They are people with rival households, not a universal enemy faction.

**Hedra of the Last Hearth** is an original divine figure associated with shelter through winter. Some communities understand her as an ancestor who became more than human; others regard that story as a poet's invention. Her keepers maintain emergency houses and resist rulers who deny refuge to strangers. A northern person may honor Hedra, follow a family guardian, worship another power, or practice no formal religion.

The main political tension is between collective survival and the prestige of heroic leaders. A celebrated raid may have made one harbor rich and left another without enough hands for the harvest. A peaceful ship captain can command as much respect as a warrior.

Signature adventure spaces: a burial ship suspended in the northern lights; a forge inside a breathing mountain; a winter hall where an absent guest's place slowly freezes over; a pass defended by ancestors who no longer recognize the living banners.

### South: Serevan and the Sunrealm

Serevan is the southern cultural sphere. The Sanctum Sunrealm is one major state within it; independent well towns and coastal ports contest its claims. Its institutions include guesthouses, observatories, water councils, craft associations, and caravan courts.

The **Unbanked Flame** are independent fire beings with households and rival courts. Some dwell in furnaces and volcanic chambers; others travel in hot winds. Human storytellers describe their oldest courts as cities that appear inside the sunset. These are the setting's own beings, drawing on the southern inspiration without making every supernatural encounter a borrowed mythological character.

**Avar, the Open Sun** is worshipped in the Sanctum as a revealer and a giver of warmth. Public rites traditionally expose wrongdoing to daylight; authoritarian priests have used that ideal to justify surveillance. Other worshippers defend private grief and argue that revelation without compassion becomes cruelty. Southern faith therefore contains meaningful internal debate.

Water infrastructure is sophisticated and indispensable. Engineers can solve problems that priests cannot, while some springs have supernatural sources that engineering alone cannot replace. Neither profession owns the whole truth.

Signature adventure spaces: a caravan shelter carried by a patient stone creature; a city visible in polished brass but absent from the surrounding desert; an underground garden watered by a dreaming spring; a fire court whose doors open only while the sun touches the horizon.

### West: Thornmere and the River Courts

Thornmere is the western landscape of waterways, orchard hills, wooded estates, and old roads. Riverside Reach is its largest trading power. River nobles compete with town charters, village commons, religious houses, and independent ferries.

The **Briar Courts** inhabit an overlapping country reached through particular groves, ruined halls, and river crossings. Their rulers differ in temperament and purpose. Some regard human towns as neighbors; others treat people as brief and replaceable tenants. They are capable of affection, cruelty, boredom, loyalty, and error.

The **Antlered Procession** crosses the western sky before certain storms. It is a moving supernatural event, not the servant of every forest ruler. Its riders pursue particular quarry. Learning who called it and what it is hunting can matter more than defeating a rider.

Western stories praise chivalry, but the meaning of service is contested. Knights can answer to a lord, a bridge community, a sanctuary, or a personal vow. A dragon that occupies a valley may be a predator, an established territorial power, or a negotiator with demands the local people cannot meet.

Signature adventure spaces: a castle visible only in the river below it; an orchard whose fruit contains fragments of future dreams; a feast that lasts one night inside and a season outside; a bridge whose reflection leads somewhere different.

### Center: Alderfall and the Hearthlands

In the revised usage, Alderfall primarily names the central realm and the larger historical alliance associated with it, not the entire planet. Characters from distant regions do not have to describe their homelands as parts of Alderfall. The game title remains *Echoes of Alderfall* because the old alliance's decisions shape the present crisis.

The Crownlands sit among craft towns, cultivated fields, and deep forests. The **Greenward** is the inhabited forest beyond the settled boundaries: immense living trees, hidden settlements, animal powers, and paths that change with the season. Some forest communities are human; others are not.

Small **Hearthkin** sometimes form around long-kept homes and workshops. They differ with the place and its history. A bakery guardian might care about the shared oven, while a shipyard presence follows a family of builders when they move. They can be playful and helpful without every encounter demanding payment or exposing a crime.

**The Keeper of the Returning Seed** is a seasonal figure honored in several central traditions. Stories disagree about whether the Keeper is one being, a changing office, or the forest's own renewal. Seasonal festivals are occasions for courtship, games, trade, and visiting family as well as ritual.

Signature adventure spaces: a village sheltered inside an enormous fallen tree; a mill whose wheel turns in a dry stream; a mining gallery beneath the roots of a walking wood; a royal archive with a room that remembers its previous occupants.

### East: The Lantern Isles and the Kasen Courts

The Isles contain competing provinces, free ports, upland communities, and sanctuary lands. **Kasen** is the name of an old court tradition shared by several provinces, not the name of every islander or a single all-powerful empire. Provincial rulers must negotiate with port assemblies, landholders, and sanctuary custodians.

The **Near Shore** is the ordinary world; the **Further Shore** is a neighboring domain entered through local crossings. These are eastern concepts with practical relevance in the Isles. They need not be the ultimate explanation of every other culture's supernatural experience.

Some cherished or neglected objects become **Remembered Things**. An old sail can remember a missing harbor; a stage mask can repeat the voice of its last performer. Mountain and river powers have territories and histories independent of these objects and of human ghosts.

The **Keeper of the Returning Tide** is one coastal power honored by ferrymen and families of the missing. It is distinct from the central Keeper of the Returning Seed despite the shared translated title. Inland communities may know little about its rites. Local sanctuaries maintain relationships with many powers rather than enforcing a uniform island-wide creed.

Signature adventure spaces: a ferry that stops at a village erased from modern maps; a mountain stair that passes through several seasons; a theater where masks dispute the ending of a play; a storm-bound harbor approached through the memory of an old sail.

### The Belltower Fenlands

The Fenlands form their own mainland culture. They share seasonal and household traditions with the center, trade with the east, and argue with western barons about river control. Their people are experts in floating construction, seasonal navigation, and communication across flooded ground.

Some bells can be heard below water. The **Deep Listeners** answer them: beings whose movements influence channels, shoals, and submerged passages. Their relationship with human bellkeepers is uneven and local. The **Drowned Choir** consists of particular human dead entangled in a past disaster; it is not another name for the Deep Listeners.

A village's flood festival can celebrate skill and survival rather than mourning. Boat races, floating gardens, reed instruments, and raised market platforms should make the region lively alongside its haunting places.

## 13. What makes the world fantasy

The world contains powerful magic, extraordinary creatures, ancient ruins, enchanted equipment, and places that cannot exist under ordinary physical laws. Folklore governs how those things behave. Social conflicts give adventures personal stakes, but they should not explain away every wonder.

For each regional story cluster, include an encounter motivated by curiosity, joy, craft, competition, or discovery. A giant can need an apprentice; a festival can require a spectacular performance; a spirit can simply enjoy company. Ordinary bandits, dangerous animals, and ambitious rulers also exist without supernatural sponsorship.

Power has several sources: learned spellcraft, relationships with beings, inherited or discovered artifacts, divine service, and the living environment. No ancestry exclusively owns a source. Classes describe a character's practice, not their ethnicity. A northern scholar, southern ranger, eastern armored defender, or western healer should all feel natural.

Do not force every creature into one moral scheme. People can distinguish a wild predator, a territorial spirit, a human criminal, and a servant of Vaelthara even when each is dangerous. Bestiary lore should explain signs, behavior, and useful preparation.

Names in narration are readable translations where appropriate. Existing surnames such as Snowroot, Glass, and Reed may be translated local bynames. Avoid simulated real-world accents and random foreign words as the main way to signal a culture.

The linked historical collections remain starting references. Research specific borrowed traditions when developing them in detail. The original inventions here can be developed freely without claiming they are authentic real-world beliefs.

## 14. Revised central premise

### The story the player enters

You follow reports of a failing road shrine and arrive as Vaelthara tears open its protection. She defeats you and deliberately leaves a survivor. You reach Oathstead among people displaced by different versions of the same disaster: the dead walking a northern pass, a southern fire ward burning its keepers, a western road leading into an impossible hunt, and eastern ferries returning without their passengers.

Maelis helps you turn a vulnerable camp into a place people can defend. What first appears to be a campaign of destruction becomes an attempt to seize the ancient network connecting the realms. To reach Vaelthara's seat at the Hollow Throne, you must recover twelve stones and learn what each one was made to do. Doing so brings you into the living stories of the regions, their people, and powers that remember the last war differently.

You are useful because you survived, witnessed the breach, and build relationships across divided communities. There is no required secret royal bloodline. A proposed magical wound from the opening lets you sense nearby breaches, but provides neither moral authority nor immunity. Its appearance and gameplay use remain implementation decisions.

### Author-facing truth: the old victory

During the Demon War, different peoples and supernatural powers joined forces against an incursion through the Hollow Throne. The twelve stones coordinated that defense. They were originally tools with distinct functions, not twelve interchangeable trophies.

As the coalition neared collapse, its leaders altered the network so some participating powers could no longer withdraw. Temporary service became permanent compulsion. Later rulers inherited safer roads and productive lands without admitting what sustained them. Some knew; others received incomplete accounts. Different communities benefited or suffered to different degrees.

The network did protect people from a real threat. Its abuses do not make every past defender a fraud or every present settlement culpable. Repairing the world requires both protecting living communities and changing the arrangements that injure others.

### Vaelthara's proposed new role

Retain the name and title **Vaelthara, Demon Queen of Mercy**. In the revised history she was a defeated power bound into the network, compelled to absorb destructive forces after the war. She has broken part of that confinement and now intends to take the network's central command.

Her offered mercy is the end of uncertainty: no winter hunger, no dangerous spirit crossings, no grief allowed to grow unchecked. The price is submission to one will. Her protected enclaves initially demonstrate real relief, followed by enforced obedience and the suppression of dissent. She can recognize suffering while choosing domination.

She leaves the player alive to spread a witnessed warning and the possibility of surrender. She also overestimates fear and underestimates voluntary cooperation. She does not secretly arrange every companion tragedy or intend every action the player takes.

### The central choice

The campaign asks whether safety can be maintained without making people and other beings permanent instruments. Oathstead provides practical evidence that cooperation can work imperfectly. The ending must still require confronting Vaelthara and overcoming the threat she actively presents; learning her history does not remove that conflict.

## 15. Campaign structure and the twelve stones

### Campaign acts

| Act | Player experience | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I — Ashes at the Road Shrine | Survive, help Maelis shelter people, investigate the destroyed shrine, and establish Oathstead. | The attack becomes a shared regional danger, not merely a private revenge quest. |
| II — The Map Beneath the Map | Visit Archive City, recover the first stone, and discover routes omitted from current maps. | The player learns that the ward network extends beyond the mainland alliance. |
| III — The Living Realms | Travel through northern, western, southern, central, Fenland, and eastern chapters; meet local powers and follow companion stories. | Each stone reveals another function of the network and another view of the old war. |
| IV — A Place Worth Defending | Resist Vaelthara's assault on Oathstead and gather representatives and witnesses. | Relationships become a visible defense, and the alliance agrees on terms for opening the Gate. |
| V — The Hollow Throne | Enter the network's ancient command site, confront Vaelthara, and resolve its future. | Local outcomes shape the capacity to maintain a new settlement after victory. |

Regional chapters can open in several orders after Archive City. The eastern voyage belongs within Act III. Oathstead development and companion stories continue between journeys. Exact gating must be designed against traversal and progression systems.

### Target stone roles

Keep the twelve existing item identities as migration anchors. The functions and proposed custody below are new fiction; several acquisition quests must change.

| Stone | Function in the old network | Target story and custody |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Memory | Preserves the identities and terms recognized by the network. | Archive City; recover a record whose omissions can be demonstrated. |
| Iron | Holds a boundary under physical or magical pressure. | Highwall; break Kharvok's control of ancestral defenders. |
| Ember | Transfers power between willing sources. | Sanctum; the forge rite reveals how transfer was changed into extraction. |
| Tides | Opens and closes routes across shifting boundaries. | Lantern Isles; recover it through a crossing controlled by a local power. **Reassigned from the existing Fenlands reward.** |
| Hunger | Draws in surplus force during a crisis. | Riverside; its corrupted use consumes harvests and sustains an unnatural hunt. |
| Roots | Distributes protection through living places. | Central orchard and Greenward; restore a route through a wounded living landscape. |
| Graves | Keeps the network from treating dead participants as living servants. | Stonegate; recover names and end a broken burial binding. |
| Frost | Slows a failing process long enough for people to intervene. | Snowrest and the northern road; lift a winter suspension trapping people as well as danger. |
| Bells | Coordinates warnings and responses across distance. | Fenlands; repair communication with the Deep Listeners and free the drowned voices. |
| Ash | Safely releases the residue of spent magic. | Blackvault; confront the accumulated effects of a century of suppressed discharge. |
| Oaths | Recognizes freely committed participants. | Oathstead defense; disparate defenders choose a shared responsibility. |
| Dawn | Restarts the whole system under an agreed configuration. | The gathering of realms; recognize the new settlement after the other eleven stones are secured. |

This preserves twelve stones and the Dawn-last structure while changing the campaign's substance. The eastern Tides chapter must replace its old reward source; the Fenlands chapter must be consolidated around Bells. Neither stone is duplicated. Companions illuminate these functions, but recruiting or romancing a particular companion must not be necessary to finish the main story.

### Proposed endings

- **Renewal:** Defeat Vaelthara and build a distributed network whose participants can renegotiate or withdraw. Prior regional agreements determine how difficult the transition is and what help Oathstead can provide.
- **Custodianship:** Defeat her and retain more of the central structure under limited, supervised authority. It offers immediate stability but leaves difficult reforms and continuing dependence.
- **Severance:** Defeat her and dismantle central command. Local protections survive where the player prepared them; other communities face a hard rebuilding period.

These are story targets, not existing selectable endings. Avoid labeling one choice perfect. Explain its concrete local consequences and show which promises the player can actually keep.

## 17. Oathstead, tone, and story boundaries

Oathstead is a crossroads settlement whose institutions emerge from its residents. A public hearth, rescue store, clinic, workshop, guest court, garden, and landing place should develop through actual needs. Regional contributions create lived spaces, not collectible national pavilions.

Residents disagree about practical matters: whether a spirit can own a room, who maintains winter stores, when a warning bell should ring, whether a gate stays open during an attack, and how much water visitors may take during a shortage. Some disputes are settled with patient compromise; others require the player to choose.

Companion friendship and romance grow through attention, disagreement, humor, work, and shared leisure. Cultural customs can offer ways to express affection, but completing a rite or quest never substitutes for an individual's consent. Friendship receives its own satisfying scenes and future plans.

Balance darkness with discovery. The story can include frightening spirits, coercion, grief, and political betrayal alongside music, strange festivals, warm kitchens, surprising friendships, and the pleasure of mastering a craft. Every companion needs a desire beyond recovering from past harm.

The rewrite succeeds when a player can recognize a region through what happens there and how people live, and recognize a companion through their choices. Descriptions, dialogue, encounters, map evidence, and quest consequences must tell the same story.

## 18. Religious life, temples, and hostile factions

Religion belongs to ordinary life as well as adventure. People visit sacred places for meals, teaching, mourning, celebration, craft, medical help, and questions whose answers remain uncertain. Faith can be sincere and sustaining even when an institution is compromised. A temple's services, authority, resources, and relationships should explain its place in the surrounding settlement.

The target institutions are northern Hearthhouses of Hedra; southern Courts of the Open Sun; western Sanctuaries of the Open Bough, honoring the Wayfarer Beneath the Bough; central Seedhouses and Hearth Chapels; Fenland Houses of the Listening Bell; and eastern Sanctuaries of the Returning Tide. These are traditions with local variation, not six uniform organizations controlling every believer in their region.

Conflicts distinguish observed supernatural effects from interpretations, religious teachings, institutions, and particular abuses. Showing that a ward works does not establish that its keeper is right about everything. An enemy may practice a familiar faith sincerely; a peaceful secret society may be called a cult by hostile neighbors.

The proposed hostile factions have specific practices: Ashen Keel binds ancestral defenders; Closed Dawn drains unwilling participants through altered fire rites; Antler's Due marks people as quarry; Still Harvest sustains unnatural growth with living anchors; Unending Vigil traps the drowned in continued service; and Unbroken Mask forcibly copies people into memorial objects. Vaelthara's Merciful Hand combines material aid with compulsory submission. Some factions accept her help; others pursue independent goals.

Members differ in responsibility and knowledge. Camps need workers, stores, recruitment, dissent, prisoners where relevant, and a reason for occupying their location. Dungeons need an original purpose and a history of alteration. A sacred symbol alone does not make a site dangerous or explain its enemies.

The detailed [faith and dungeon specification](faith-cults-and-dungeons.md) defines six temple traditions, regional camps and dungeons, and the proposed **Guest Below the Flame** story. It links public religious life to a specific investigation and confrontation while keeping ordinary believers and independent fire beings distinct from the hostile Closed Dawn leadership.

## 19. Beasts and regional powers

The wilderness is inhabited by creatures whose lives extend beyond human politics. Territorial predators, intelligent giants, forest guardians, serpents, wandering spirits, and the returned dead provide different kinds of danger and wonder. Human factions sometimes exploit them; neither cults nor Vaelthara explain their entire existence.

Northern jötunn-inspired beings have scale, power, territory, and individual motives. Southern creatures include great nesting rocs, dangerous ghouls, and independent fire powers alongside desert animals. Eastern river and crossing ecologies support both vast many-headed serpents and smaller, uncanny predators. Western wyrms, central living guardians, and Fenland channel beasts give neighboring regions their own encounters.

The proposed signature bosses are:

| Region | Bosses | Distinct story function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| North | Hrold, the Jötunn of the Broken Pass; Eydra, Captain of the Unmoored. | Reopen a mountain supply route; release or defeat a compelled ancestral ship. |
| South | Whitewing, the Roc of the Salt Cliffs; Vahrun, Lord of the Sealed Furnace; the Borrowed Host. | Resolve a nesting conflict; confront a fire lord taking a town's water; hunt a predatory ghoul. |
| West | Thornmaw, the Orchard Wyrm. | Restore farms and a route disrupted by an established territorial dragon. |
| Center | Rootmaw, the Stag Beneath the Orchard. | Resolve the existing guardian and Stone of Roots story through a fuller encounter. |
